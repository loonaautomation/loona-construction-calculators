🧵 Wire Size Calculator

Tell this calculator the current, the one-way run length, the supply voltage and the voltage drop you can accept, and it works out the minimum cable cross-section — then rounds up to the nearest standard size in mm² with its AWG equivalent.

How to use the Wire Size Calculator

  1. Enter the current the load draws and the one-way distance from supply to load.
  2. Enter the supply voltage and the drop you can allow — 3% is the common guide for lighting, 5% for other circuits.
  3. Choose copper or aluminium and single-phase or three-phase, then press Calculate for the minimum and the nearest standard size.
Example: 16 A over 20 m of copper at 230 V with 3% allowed: the minimum is 1.56 mm², so the nearest standard size is 2.5 mm² (≈ 14 AWG).

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is the answer rounded up to a standard size?

Cable is manufactured in standard cross-sections. Rounding down to the size below the calculated minimum would exceed your drop limit, so the calculator always rounds up — and the result box shows the (lower) drop the standard size actually gives.

Does this calculator check that the cable can carry the current?

No — and that matters. This tool solves the voltage-drop half of cable sizing only. Ampacity (safe current-carrying capacity) depends on how the cable is installed — in free air, conduit, insulation or underground — and on temperature and grouping. Check the size against your local code's ampacity tables, or ask an electrician, and use the larger of the two sizes.

Why does aluminium need a thicker cable than copper?

Aluminium's resistivity is about 1.58× copper's, so for the same drop limit it needs roughly 58% more cross-section — in practice one or two standard steps up. Aluminium also needs suitable terminations; follow local code for its use.

How it works — the formula

Allowed drop (V) = Supply voltage × Allowed % ÷ 100
Single-phase: A = 2 × I × ρ × L ÷ Allowed drop
Three-phase: A = √3 × I × ρ × L ÷ Allowed drop

with ρ = 0.0168 Ω·mm²/m for copper (1.68 × 10−8 Ω·m) and 0.0265 Ω·mm²/m for aluminium (2.65 × 10−8 Ω·m), L the one-way length in metres and A the cross-section in mm². The raw minimum is then rounded up to the next standard size (1.5, 2.5, 4, 6, 10, 16, 25, 35, 50, 70, 95, 120, 150, 185, 240 mm²), and the AWG figure is the nearest common American gauge to that standard size — an approximation, since the two systems' steps do not line up exactly.

Worked example

Copper, single-phase, I = 16 A, L = 20 m, 230 V, 3% allowed. Allowed drop = 230 × 0.03 = 6.9 V. A = 2 × 16 × 0.0168 × 20 ÷ 6.9 = 10.752 ÷ 6.9 = 1.56 mm². The next standard size at or above 1.56 mm² is 2.5 mm² (≈ 14 AWG), which in fact drops only 2 × 16 × 0.0168 × 20 ÷ 2.5 = 4.30 V (1.87%).
Safety disclaimer: this calculator sizes a cable for voltage drop only. It does not check current-carrying capacity (ampacity), which depends on the installation method, grouping, insulation and temperature, nor protective-device coordination or earthing. Those checks come from your local wiring code's tables and a qualified electrician — never install a cable on the strength of a voltage-drop result alone, and always use the larger size when the two checks disagree.

Frequently asked questions

Is the AWG equivalent exact?

No. AWG sizes (…3.31 mm² for 12 AWG, 5.26 mm² for 10 AWG…) do not coincide with metric standard sizes, so the figure shown is the nearest common gauge. If you are buying by AWG and the equivalent sits between gauges, choose the thicker (lower-numbered) gauge.

What drop percentage should I allow?

Common guidance (IEC 60364-5-52 informative annex and national rules based on it) is about 3% for lighting and 5% for other uses, from the origin of the installation. If part of that budget is already used by an upstream run, allow only what remains for this run.

The result says larger than 240 mm² — what then?

Very long or heavy runs are normally solved with parallel cables, a higher supply voltage, or moving the supply closer to the load. That is specialist design territory — bring in a qualified electrician or electrical engineer.
