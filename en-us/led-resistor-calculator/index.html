💡 LED Resistor Calculator

An LED connected straight to a supply will burn out — it needs a series resistor to set its current. Enter the supply voltage, the LED's forward voltage and current, and how many LEDs are in series, and get the resistor value, the nearest standard size and the wattage it must handle.

How to use the LED Resistor Calculator

  1. Enter the supply voltage (5 V USB, 9 V battery, 12 V car supply…).
  2. Enter the LED's forward voltage from its datasheet — or use the typical values in the label — and its rated forward current, usually 20 mA.
  3. If several LEDs are wired in series behind one resistor, enter how many, then press Calculate.
Example: A red LED (Vf 2.0 V, 20 mA) on a 5 V supply: the resistor must drop 3.0 V, so R = 3.0 ÷ 0.020 = 150 Ω — itself a standard value.

Frequently Asked Questions

What forward voltage does my LED have?

From its datasheet ideally. Typical ranges: red 1.8–2.2 V, orange/yellow 2.0–2.2 V, green 2.2–3.2 V (older green ~2.2, modern high-brightness ~3), blue and white 3.0–3.4 V, infrared ~1.2–1.5 V. When in doubt for a small indicator LED, 2.0 V (red) or 3.2 V (white/blue) are safe planning figures.

Why can't I connect an LED directly to a battery?

An LED's current rises explosively with voltage once past its forward voltage — a fraction of a volt too much can mean ten times the current, overheating and failure. The series resistor turns that cliff into a controlled slope.

Can I put LEDs in parallel on one resistor?

It is poor practice: no two LEDs have exactly the same forward voltage, so one hogs current, runs hotter, its Vf falls further, and it hogs more — thermal runaway until it fails. Give each parallel LED (or series string) its own resistor.

How it works — the formula

Voltage across the resistor = Supply V − (LEDs in series × Forward V)
Resistance (Ω) = Voltage across resistor ÷ Current (A)
Resistor power (W) = Actual current² × Standard resistor value

An LED's brightness is set by its current, and its forward voltage is roughly fixed by its chemistry — so the resistor's job is to absorb the leftover voltage at exactly the wanted current. The result is rounded up to the next standard E12 value (…100, 120, 150, 180, 220, 270, 330, 390, 470…), which can only make the current slightly lower and safer, and the resistor's wattage rating should be at least twice the power it actually dissipates.

Worked example

Supply 5 V, one red LED with Vf = 2.0 V at 20 mA. Voltage across resistor = 5 − 2.0 = 3.0 V. R = 3.0 ÷ 0.020 = 150 Ω, which is itself an E12 standard value, so the actual current stays 20.0 mA. Power in the resistor = 0.020² × 150 = 0.06 W — a common 0.25 W resistor gives a comfortable margin.

Frequently asked questions

What if the calculated value is between standard sizes?

Always round up to the next E12 value. The LED runs a little below its rated current — brightness drops only slightly (the eye is logarithmic) while lifetime and reliability improve.

How big a resistor wattage do I need?

At least twice the calculated dissipation as a rule of thumb, so the resistor runs cool and its value stays stable. For the common 5 V / 20 mA case almost any 0.25 W resistor is ample; for 12 V supplies or power LEDs, check the figure the calculator shows.

Does this work for LED strips and high-power LEDs?

LED strips have resistors built in — just supply the rated voltage. High-power LEDs (1 W and up) are normally driven by a constant-current driver rather than a resistor, because a resistor wastes a lot of power and the current drifts as the LED warms up.
