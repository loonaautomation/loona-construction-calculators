🔺 Three-Phase Power Calculator

For a balanced three-phase load, enter the line-to-line voltage, the line current and the power factor — and get the real power in kW, the apparent power in kVA and the reactive power in kvar, all from the standard √3 formulas.

How to use the Three-Phase Power Calculator

  1. Enter the line-to-line voltage of the system — 400 V in most of Europe, 480 V or 208 V in North America.
  2. Enter the line current (what a clamp meter on any one line reads, for a balanced load).
  3. Enter the load's power factor, then press Calculate.
Example: 400 V, 16 A per line, PF 0.85: apparent power = √3 × 400 × 16 = 11.09 kVA, real power = 9.42 kW.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why √3 and not just 3?

Because the line-to-line voltage is itself √3 times the line-to-neutral voltage (400 V = √3 × 230 V). Using √3 × V(line-line) × I counts all three phases exactly once. If you mistakenly use 3 × 400 × I you overstate the power by 73%.

What if my load is unbalanced?

Then a single √3 calculation no longer applies — work out each phase separately as a single-phase load (V line-to-neutral × that phase's current × its PF) and add the three. Unbalance also loads the neutral, which balanced-load math assumes is idle.

What power factor do three-phase motors have?

A loaded induction motor typically runs at 0.8–0.9; lightly loaded it can fall to 0.6 or below. The nameplate or a power-quality meter gives the real figure — the current changes a lot with PF, the kW much less.

How it works — the formula

Apparent power: S (kVA) = √3 × VL-L × I ÷ 1,000
Real power: P (kW) = √3 × VL-L × I × PF ÷ 1,000
Reactive power: Q (kvar) = √(S² − P²)

The √3 appears because the three phases are 120° apart: the total is not 3 × V × I with the line voltage, but √3 × the line-to-line voltage × the line current. Equivalently, each phase delivers VL-N × I × PF (230 V × I × PF on a 400/230 V system), and the three phases sum to the same total.

Worked example

V = 400, I = 16 A, PF = 0.85. S = √3 × 400 × 16 ÷ 1,000 = 11,085 ÷ 1,000 = 11.09 kVA. P = 11.09 × 0.85 = 9.42 kW. Q = √(11.09² − 9.42²) = √(122.88 − 88.78) = √34.10 = 5.84 kvar.

Frequently asked questions

How do I go backwards — kW to amps?

Rearrange: I = kW × 1,000 ÷ (√3 × V × PF). The Watts to Amps Calculator has a three-phase option that does exactly this, and the Motor Current Calculator adds motor efficiency on top.

What is kvar in plain terms?

The magnetising power that motors and transformers borrow and return every cycle. It does no work but it does occupy current-carrying capacity — which is why correcting it with capacitors (see the Power Factor Calculator) frees up supply capacity.

Is 400 V the phase or the line voltage?

400 V is line-to-line; each phase sits at 230 V to neutral on that system. This calculator wants the line-to-line figure with the line current — the pair a clamp meter and the system name give you.
