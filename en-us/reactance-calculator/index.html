〰️ Reactance Calculator

How much does a capacitor or inductor oppose AC at a given frequency? Enter the frequency and a capacitance and/or inductance, and get Xc and Xl in ohms — with both components, you also get the net reactance and the resonant frequency where they cancel.

How to use the Reactance Calculator

  1. Enter the frequency in hertz (mains is 50 or 60 Hz; audio runs to 20,000 Hz).
  2. Enter the capacitance in µF and/or the inductance in mH — one is enough, both unlock the resonance result.
  3. Press Calculate for the reactances in ohms.
Example: At 1,000 Hz: a 1 µF capacitor has Xc = 159.15 Ω; a 10 mH inductor has Xl = 62.83 Ω; together they resonate at 1,591.5 Hz.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between reactance and resistance?

Both are measured in ohms and both oppose current, but resistance converts electrical energy to heat, while ideal reactance stores energy (in an electric or magnetic field) and returns it each cycle. In a real AC circuit the two combine as impedance, Z = √(R² + X²).

Why does a capacitor block DC but pass AC?

At DC (f = 0) the formula gives Xc = infinity — once charged, no current flows. As frequency rises the capacitor charges and discharges ever faster, so its effective opposition falls toward zero. That is why capacitors couple audio between amplifier stages while blocking their different DC voltages.

What happens at the resonant frequency?

In a series LC circuit the reactances cancel and only the resistance limits the current — current peaks sharply. In a parallel LC circuit the opposite happens and impedance peaks. Tuners, filters, induction heaters and metal detectors all exploit one of the two behaviors.

How it works — the formula

Capacitive reactance: XC = 1 ÷ (2πfC)
Inductive reactance: XL = 2πfL
Resonant frequency: f₀ = 1 ÷ (2π√(LC))

The two components pull frequency in opposite directions: a capacitor's reactance falls as frequency rises (it passes high frequencies and blocks DC entirely), while an inductor's reactance rises with frequency (it passes DC freely and blocks high frequencies). At exactly one frequency — f₀ — they are equal and cancel, which is how radio tuners select one station and how filters are designed.

Worked example

f = 1,000 Hz, C = 1 µF = 10⁻⁶ F: XC = 1 ÷ (2π × 1,000 × 10⁻⁶) = 1 ÷ 0.006283 = 159.15 Ω. L = 10 mH = 0.01 H: XL = 2π × 1,000 × 0.01 = 62.83 Ω. Resonance: f₀ = 1 ÷ (2π × √(0.01 × 10⁻⁶)) = 1 ÷ (2π × 10⁻⁴) = 1,591.5 Hz — above 1,000 Hz the pair is net capacitive, below it likewise, and exactly at f₀ the net reactance is zero.

Frequently asked questions

How do I handle nF, pF or µH values?

Convert first: 1 µF = 1,000 nF = 1,000,000 pF, so 10 nF = 0.01 µF. For inductance, 1 mH = 1,000 µH, so 100 µH = 0.1 mH. Enter the converted figure in the µF / mH boxes.

Does reactance explain motor power factor?

Largely, yes: a motor's windings are inductors, so its current lags the voltage — that lag is the power factor story told by the Power Factor Calculator. Capacitors are deliberately added to cancel inductive reactance at the supply frequency.

Are real capacitors and inductors ideal?

No — real parts add equivalent series resistance (ESR), and inductors have winding resistance and core losses. For back-of-envelope and most design-first-pass work the ideal formulas here are the standard starting point.
