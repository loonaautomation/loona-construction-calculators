🎚️ Power Factor Calculator

Enter a load's real power in kW and its apparent power in kVA and get the power factor, the reactive power in kvar and the phase angle — and, if you set a target, the size of correction capacitor (in kvar) that would get you there.

How to use the Power Factor Calculator

  1. Enter the real power in kW — from a power meter, or an appliance's rating.
  2. Enter the apparent power in kVA: volts × amps ÷ 1,000 (single-phase), √3 × line volts × amps ÷ 1,000 (three-phase), or read it from a meter that shows kVA.
  3. Optionally set a target power factor (0.95 is a common goal) to get the correction capacitor size in kvar, then press Calculate.
Example: A load taking 100 kW at 125 kVA has PF = 100 ÷ 125 = 0.80, with 75 kvar of reactive power sloshing back and forth doing no work.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good power factor?

1.0 is perfect and typical of resistive loads. 0.95 or better is the usual target for industrial installations; many utilities start penalizing below 0.9 or 0.95 on commercial tariffs. Small motors running lightly loaded can fall to 0.6–0.7.

Why does a low power factor cost money?

The supply, cables and transformer carry current in proportion to kVA, not kW. At PF 0.8 a 100 kW load needs the infrastructure of a 125 kVA load — more losses, bigger equipment, and often an explicit low-PF charge on commercial bills.

How do I find my kVA if I only know volts and amps?

Single-phase: kVA = V × A ÷ 1,000. Three-phase: kVA = √3 × V(line-line) × A ÷ 1,000. The kVA to Amps Calculator does the same math in reverse.

How it works — the formula

Power factor: PF = kW ÷ kVA = cos φ
Reactive power: Q = √(kVA² − kW²)
Correction capacitor: Qc = kW × (tan φ1 − tan φ2), where φ1 is the present angle and φ2 the target angle.

The three powers form a right-angled triangle: kW along the base (useful work), kvar vertical (magnetising energy that motors and transformers cycle back to the supply), and kVA as the hypotenuse (what the supply actually carries). The power factor is the cosine of the angle between kW and kVA — 1.0 is a flat triangle with no waste, and every step below 1 means more current for the same work.

Worked example

kW = 100, kVA = 125. PF = 100 ÷ 125 = 0.80. Reactive power: Q = √(125² − 100²) = √(15,625 − 10,000) = √5,625 = 75 kvar. Phase angle = cos⁻¹(0.80) = 36.87°. Correcting to 0.95: Qc = 100 × (tan 36.87° − tan 18.19°) = 100 × (0.750 − 0.329) = 42.13 kvar of capacitors.

Frequently asked questions

Can a capacitor really fix it?

Yes — inductive loads (motors, transformers, fluorescent ballasts) consume lagging reactive power, and capacitors supply leading reactive power. Correctly sized, automatically switched capacitor banks are standard industrial equipment. They must be specified properly: over-correction can cause its own problems, and harmonics may need detuned reactors.

Does power factor matter at home?

Bills for homes are almost everywhere charged on kWh (real energy), so a poor PF costs you little directly — though the extra current still heats your wiring. It matters most for businesses on kVA or demand tariffs.

Is power factor the same as efficiency?

No. Efficiency is useful output ÷ electrical input (a motor's shaft kW ÷ its electrical kW). Power factor is real ÷ apparent power at the input. A motor can be 90% efficient and still have a 0.8 power factor.
