🔌 Amps to Watts Calculator

How many watts is that current draw? Enter the amps and volts, choose DC, single-phase or three-phase, set the power factor for AC motor-type loads, and get the real power in watts and kilowatts — plus the apparent power in kVA.

How to use the Amps to Watts Calculator

  1. Choose the system: DC, AC single-phase, or AC three-phase.
  2. Enter the current in amps and the voltage — for three-phase, the line-to-line voltage (e.g. 400 V).
  3. For AC, enter the power factor: 1.0 for heaters and other resistive loads, typically 0.75–0.9 for motors. DC ignores the power factor. Press Calculate.
Example: 10 A at 230 V single-phase with a power factor of 0.8: P = 230 × 10 × 0.8 = 1,840 W.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do I need the voltage to turn amps into watts?

Amps measure flow, watts measure power — and power is flow × pressure (voltage). 10 A at 12 V is only 120 W, while 10 A at 230 V is 2,300 W. There is no single amps-to-watts conversion without a voltage.

What power factor should I use?

1.0 for resistive loads (heaters, kettles, incandescent lamps) and for DC. Motors, compressors and fluorescent or LED drivers run lower — 0.75–0.9 is typical for motors; the load's nameplate or datasheet gives its actual figure.

What is the difference between watts and VA / kVA?

Watts are real power — the part that does work and appears on your bill. Volt-amps are apparent power — watts divided by the power factor — and are what cables, breakers, transformers and generators must be sized for.

How it works — the formula

DC: P = V × I
AC single-phase: P = V × I × PF
AC three-phase: P = √3 × VL-L × I × PF
Apparent power: S = V × I (single-phase / DC) or √3 × VL-L × I (three-phase), shown in kVA.

Amps alone do not tell you power — the same 10 A is 120 W on a 12 V DC circuit, 2,300 W on a 230 V resistive circuit, and over 5.5 kW on a 400 V three-phase supply. That is why the voltage, the system type and (for AC) the power factor all matter.

Worked example

Single-phase, I = 10 A, V = 230 V, PF = 0.8: P = 230 × 10 × 0.8 = 1,840 W (1.84 kW). The apparent power is 230 × 10 = 2,300 VA = 2.30 kVA — the difference between the two is the power factor at work.

Frequently asked questions

How do I do this for a three-phase load?

Use the three-phase option with the line-to-line voltage (400 V in most of Europe, 480 V / 208 V in North America) and the line current. The √3 factor in the formula accounts for the three phases sharing the load.

Can I get kilowatts directly?

Yes — the result shows watts and kilowatts together (watts ÷ 1,000). For billing and comparing with motor or generator ratings, kW is usually the handier unit.

Is 1 amp always 230 watts in Europe?

Only on a 230 V single-phase circuit with a power factor of 1. With a motor at PF 0.8 the same amp is 184 W of real power — a common source of undersized estimates.
