⚡ Ohm's Law Calculator

Enter any two values — voltage, current, resistance or power — leave the other two blank, and this calculator works out the rest using Ohm's law and the power law. (No unit switch here: volts, amps, ohms and watts are the same in metric and imperial countries.)

How to use the Ohm's Law Calculator

  1. Decide which two values you know — for example the supply voltage and the current a device draws.
  2. Type those two in and leave the other two boxes empty.
  3. Press Calculate: the two unknown values are worked out, and all four are shown together so you can check them against each other.
Example: A 12 V supply feeding a circuit that draws 3 A: resistance = 12 ÷ 3 = 4 Ω and power = 12 × 3 = 36 W.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Ohm's law in simple words?

It says the current through a conductor is proportional to the voltage across it and inversely proportional to its resistance: double the voltage and (for the same resistor) you double the current; double the resistance and the current halves. It is written V = I × R.

Which two values should I enter?

Any two of the four. Common pairs: voltage + current (to get resistance and power, e.g. for checking a load), voltage + power (to get the current a device will draw — the pair you need before choosing a fuse or cable), and current + resistance (to get the voltage dropped across a component).

Why must I leave two boxes blank?

With three or four values entered the problem is over-specified and the values may contradict each other. Exactly two knowns have exactly one consistent answer for the other two — that is the whole point of the Ohm's law wheel.

How it works — the formula

Ohm's law: V = I × R  ·  I = V ÷ R  ·  R = V ÷ I
Power law: P = V × I  ·  P = I² × R  ·  P = V² ÷ R
Square-root forms (from R and P): V = √(P × R)  ·  I = √(P ÷ R)

Picture the Ohm's law wheel: four quantities — V at the top, then I, R and P — arranged in a circle of twelve formulas. Cover the quantity you want with a finger and the two visible neighbours tell you the formula: cover V and you see I × R; cover I and you see V ÷ R (and P ÷ V); cover R and you see V ÷ I (and P ÷ I²); cover P and you see V × I, I² × R or V² ÷ R. Whichever pair you start with, the wheel closes: the two unknowns fall out of the two you know, which is exactly what this calculator does for all four quadrants.

Worked example

A circuit draws I = 2 A through a resistance of R = 47 Ω. Voltage: V = I × R = 2 × 47 = 94 V. Power: P = I² × R = 2² × 47 = 4 × 47 = 188 W. Cross-check with the other power formula: P = V × I = 94 × 2 = 188 W — both routes agree, as they must.

Frequently asked questions

Does Ohm's law work for AC?

For purely resistive AC loads (heaters, incandescent lamps) yes, using RMS voltage and current. For motors, transformers and other inductive or capacitive loads, impedance replaces simple resistance and real power also involves the power factor, so treat results for those loads as a first approximation.

How do I find the current an appliance draws?

Enter its rated power and the supply voltage. Example: a 2,300 W kettle on 230 V draws I = P ÷ V = 2,300 ÷ 230 = 10 A — which is why it needs a circuit and plug rated above 10 A.

What if my resistance is in kilo-ohms or my current in milliamps?

Convert first: 1 kΩ = 1,000 Ω and 1 mA = 0.001 A. Entering 500 mA as 0.5 and 2 kΩ as 2000 keeps the maths right; the results come back in volts, amps, ohms and watts.
