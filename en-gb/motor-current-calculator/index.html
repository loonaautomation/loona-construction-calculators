🌀 Motor Current Calculator

Estimate a motor's full-load current from its rated power — in kW or horsepower — its voltage, phase, efficiency and power factor. Useful for early planning and sanity checks; the nameplate and code tables remain the authority, as the notes below explain.

How to use the Motor Current Calculator

  1. Enter the motor's rated shaft power from its plate, choosing kW or horsepower.
  2. Choose single-phase or three-phase and enter the supply voltage (line-to-line for three-phase).
  3. Enter the nameplate efficiency and full-load power factor if you have them — otherwise the defaults (90%, 0.85) suit a typical modern motor — then press Calculate.
Example: A 7.5 kW three-phase motor on 400 V at 90% efficiency and PF 0.85 draws an estimated 14.15 A at full load — close to the ~15 A a real plate would show.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is the estimate not exact?

Efficiency and power factor vary with the individual motor's design and with its load — a motor at half load draws less current but at a worse power factor. Two motors with the same kW rating from different makers can differ by 10% in full-load amps, which is why standards insist on the nameplate figure.

What if I only know the horsepower?

Choose the hp option — the calculator converts at 1 hp = 0.7457 kW. (For converting power units on their own, Loona Calculators has dedicated horsepower ↔ kilowatt converters; here the conversion is just the first step of the current estimate.)

What efficiency and power factor should I assume?

Modern three-phase motors: efficiency roughly 80% for small motors rising to 93–95% for large premium-efficiency ones; full-load PF typically 0.8–0.87 for small motors and 0.85–0.92 for large. The nameplate or datasheet beats any assumption.

How it works — the formula

Electrical input power = Shaft power ÷ Efficiency
Single-phase: I = Input W ÷ (V × PF)
Three-phase: I = Input W ÷ (√3 × VL-L × PF)
Horsepower: kW = hp × 0.7457

The step most quick estimates miss is the first one: a motor's advertised kW or hp is the mechanical power at its shaft. The electrical side must supply that plus the motor's losses, so the input is the shaft power divided by the efficiency — a 7.5 kW motor at 90% efficiency actually takes 8.33 kW from the supply before the power factor is even considered.

Worked example

7.5 kW shaft power, efficiency 90% → input = 7.5 ÷ 0.90 = 8.33 kW. Three-phase at 400 V, PF 0.85: I = 8,330 ÷ (√3 × 400 × 0.85) = 8,330 ÷ 589.0 = 14.15 A. Compare a typical real 7.5 kW, 400 V motor plate: about 15 A — the estimate is in the right place, but the plate wins whenever the two differ.
Important: this is an engineering estimate, not a design value. Motor circuits must be sized from the nameplate full-load amps and your local code's motor rules (which also cover starting current of roughly 6–8× full load for direct-on-line starting, overload protection and disconnects). Have motor installations designed or checked by a qualified electrician.

Frequently asked questions

How much current does the motor draw when starting?

Direct-on-line (DOL) starting typically draws 6–8× full-load current for a few seconds — the reason lights dip and why breakers for motor circuits use motor-rated trip curves. Star-delta starting cuts it to about a third; soft starters and VFDs can limit it to 1–1.5×.

Can I use this current to choose the cable and breaker?

Only as a first estimate. Cable and protection for motors follow specific code rules (in the US, NEC Article 430 uses standardised full-load current tables rather than the nameplate for conductor sizing, and allows higher breaker ratings to ride through starting). A qualified electrician applies those rules to the actual nameplate.

Does a VFD change the current?

On the motor side the VFD supplies whatever the motor needs; on the supply side the drive draws current with its own characteristics and harmonics. Size the supply from the drive maker's input-current rating, not from this estimate.
