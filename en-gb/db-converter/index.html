🔊 dB Converter

Decibels compress huge ratios into handy numbers. Convert a power ratio or a voltage ratio to dB against any reference you choose, or translate between dBm and real power in milliwatts and watts — the language of audio, radio and amplifier gains.

How to use the dB Converter

  1. Choose the conversion: a power or voltage ratio to dB, dBm to real power, or real power to dBm.
  2. For ratios, enter the value and its reference in the same units (watts with watts, volts with volts) — the reference is what '0 dB' means for your measurement.
  3. Press Calculate. Only the fields for the chosen mode are used.
Example: An amplifier delivering 100 W against a 1 W reference is 10 × log₁₀(100) = +20 dB; and 20 dBm is exactly 100 mW.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why are there two formulas — 10 log and 20 log?

Decibels are defined on power. Power ratios use 10 × log. Since power is proportional to voltage squared (at the same impedance), the square turns into a factor of two in the log — hence 20 × log for voltage and current ratios. Mixing them up is the most common dB mistake.

What is dBm, and how is it different from plain dB?

Plain dB is a ratio — it needs a 'compared to what'. dBm fixes the reference at 1 milliwatt, so it expresses an absolute power: a transmitter at +30 dBm puts out 1 W; a received signal at −90 dBm is a billionth of a milliwatt. dBW does the same against 1 W.

What does a negative dB or dBm value mean?

A negative ratio in dB means the value is smaller than its reference (half the power = −3.01 dB). A negative dBm means a power below 1 mW — normal and expected for received radio signals, which are routinely −70 to −110 dBm.

How it works — the formula

Power ratio: dB = 10 × log₁₀(P ÷ Pref)
Voltage ratio: dB = 20 × log₁₀(V ÷ Vref) — the 20 appears because power goes as voltage squared, and it assumes both voltages see the same impedance
dBm to power: P (mW) = 10(dBm ÷ 10)  ·  Power to dBm: dBm = 10 × log₁₀(P in mW)
dBW = dBm − 30 (referenced to 1 W instead of 1 mW)

Reference points worth memorising: +3 dB ≈ double the power, +6 dB ≈ double the voltage (four times the power), +10 dB = exactly ten times the power, and 0 dBm = 1 mW — the standard reference across radio and telecom work.

Worked example

Power ratio: P = 100, reference = 1 (same units). dB = 10 × log₁₀(100 ÷ 1) = 10 × 2 = +20 dB. Voltage check: a ratio of 10 gives 20 × log₁₀(10) = +20 dB too — because ten times the voltage across the same impedance is a hundred times the power. dBm check: 10(20 ÷ 10) = 10² = 100 mW for 20 dBm, and 30 dBm = 1,000 mW = 1 W.

Frequently asked questions

How do amplifier gains in dB combine?

They add — that is the point of logarithms. A preamp with +20 dB followed by a power amp with +26 dB gives +46 dB overall; a cable losing 3 dB subtracts 3. Working in dB turns a chain of multiplications into simple addition and subtraction.

Is the voltage-ratio rule always valid?

Only when both voltages are measured across the same impedance — true inside most audio and RF chains. Comparing a voltage at a 50 Ω point with one at a 600 Ω point by 20 log alone misstates the power ratio; convert through power when impedances differ.

How does dB relate to sound levels I see quoted?

Sound pressure level uses dB SPL referenced to 20 µPa (the threshold of hearing), with voltage-style 20 log because it is a pressure ratio. This converter handles electrical ratios and dBm; SPL is the same mathematics with a different fixed reference.
