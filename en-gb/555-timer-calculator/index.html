⏱️ 555 Timer Calculator

The 555 has been flashing LEDs and timing pulses since 1972. Choose astable (free-running oscillator) or monostable (one-shot), enter the resistors in ohms and the capacitor in µF, and get the frequency, duty cycle or pulse width.

How to use the 555 Timer Calculator

  1. Choose astable for a repeating square wave, monostable for a single timed pulse.
  2. Enter the resistors in ohms (10 kΩ = 10,000) and the capacitor in microfarads — the units most 555 circuits are drawn in.
  3. Press Calculate: astable gives frequency, duty cycle and the HIGH and LOW times; monostable gives the pulse width.
Example: Astable with R1 = 1 kΩ, R2 = 10 kΩ, C = 10 µF: f = 1.44 ÷ ((1,000 + 20,000) × 10 µF) = 6.86 Hz, duty cycle 52.4%.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why can't the standard astable circuit go below 50% duty?

The timing capacitor charges through R1 + R2 but discharges through R2 only, so the HIGH time is always the longer of the two. The classic fixes: a diode across R2 so charge bypasses it, taking the output from a different point, or following the 555 with an inverter if you simply need the opposite polarity.

What resistor and capacitor ranges work well?

Keep R1 and R2 roughly between 1 kΩ and 1 MΩ, and the capacitor between about 100 pF (0.0001 µF) and a few hundred µF. Very large electrolytics leak enough to ruin long timings; very small capacitors are swamped by stray capacitance on the board.

Does the supply voltage change the frequency?

Almost not at all — the 555 compares the capacitor voltage against ⅓ and ⅔ of whatever supply it is given, so the fractions cancel. Temperature and component tolerances (electrolytics are often ±20%) matter far more in practice.

How it works — the formula

Astable: f = 1.44 ÷ ((R1 + 2 × R2) × C)  ·  Duty = (R1 + R2) ÷ (R1 + 2 × R2) × 100%
tHIGH = 0.693 × (R1 + R2) × C  ·  tLOW = 0.693 × R2 × C
Monostable: T = 1.1 × R × C

The constants come from the 555's internal thresholds at ⅓ and ⅔ of the supply: 0.693 is ln 2, 1.44 is 1 ÷ ln 2, and 1.1 is ln 3 (the time to charge from 0 to ⅔). Because both thresholds are fractions of the supply voltage, the timing is (famously) almost independent of the supply voltage itself — a 555 circuit timed on 5 V keeps its timing on 12 V.

Worked example

Astable: R1 = 1,000 Ω, R2 = 10,000 Ω, C = 10 µF = 0.00001 F. Denominator: (1,000 + 2 × 10,000) × 0.00001 = 21,000 × 0.00001 = 0.21. f = 1.44 ÷ 0.21 = 6.86 Hz. Duty = (1,000 + 10,000) ÷ 21,000 = 52.4%. Monostable check: R = 10,000 Ω, C = 100 µF → T = 1.1 × 10,000 × 0.0001 = 1.1 s.

Frequently asked questions

How accurate are these formulas?

They are the datasheet formulas (using 1.44 / 0.693 / 1.1) and are as accurate as your components: a 1% resistor with a 10% film capacitor gives a few-percent result; a ±20% electrolytic gives a ±20% result. For precision timing, measure and trim, or use a crystal-based timer.

What is the difference between the bipolar 555 and CMOS versions?

CMOS versions (TLC555, LMC555, ICM7555) run at lower power and higher frequency and have gentler output behaviour, but the timing formulas are identical. The bipolar NE555 can source/sink about 200 mA at the output; CMOS types manage less — check the datasheet for yours.

Can a 555 drive a relay or motor directly?

A bipolar 555 output handles up to roughly 200 mA, enough for a small relay coil (with a flyback diode across it). Motors and bigger loads need a transistor or MOSFET stage — the 555 provides the timing, the transistor provides the muscle.
