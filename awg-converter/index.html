📏 AWG Converter

Convert American Wire Gauge to the numbers the rest of the world uses: diameter in mm and inches, cross-section in mm², and resistance per kilometre for copper and aluminium — or go the other way, from mm² or diameter to the nearest AWG.

How to use the AWG Converter

  1. Choose what you are starting from: an AWG size, a metric cross-section, or a measured diameter.
  2. Enter or select the value — for diameter, measure the bare conductor (calipers, insulation off, or a wire-gauge tool).
  3. Press Calculate for the full row: diameter, area and resistance per km in copper and aluminium.
Example: 12 AWG: diameter 2.053 mm (0.0808 in), area 3.31 mm², copper resistance 5.08 Ω/km at 20 °C.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why doesn't AWG match mm² steps?

AWG comes from the number of drawing dies a wire was pulled through in the 19th century — higher number, thinner wire. Its sizes (…3.31, 5.26, 8.37 mm²…) simply land between the metric preferred sizes (…2.5, 4, 6, 10 mm²…), so every conversion is a nearest match, not an equality.

If I need at least a certain mm², which AWG do I pick?

Pick the AWG whose area is at or above your requirement. Example: needing 2.5 mm², 14 AWG gives only 2.08 mm² (not enough) — 13 AWG at 2.62 mm² is the first size that covers it. This converter shows the nearest by area; for a minimum requirement, step one gauge thicker if the nearest is below your figure.

Does this converter give ampacity (how many amps a wire can carry)?

No — deliberately. Ampacity depends on the insulation type, installation method, grouping and ambient temperature, and is defined by your local wiring code's tables, not by the gauge alone. This page converts sizes and resistance only; use the Wire Size and Voltage Drop calculators for drop, and your code (or an electrician) for ratings.

How it works — the formula

AWG is a geometric series: each step changes the diameter by a fixed ratio (about ×1.123), so area roughly doubles every 3 gauges and diameter doubles every 6. The diameter and area figures here are the standard nominal values for each gauge (the series standardised in ASTM B 258, rounded for display).

Resistance per km = ρ × 1,000 ÷ Area (mm²), computed with ρ = 0.0168 Ω·mm²/m for copper and 0.0265 Ω·mm²/m for aluminium at 20 °C — the resistivity constants used consistently across this site's electrical calculators. Real conductors vary a little with stranding, temper and temperature (copper's resistance rises about 0.39% per °C).

Worked example

12 AWG from the table: diameter 2.053 mm, area 3.31 mm². Copper resistance = 16.8 ÷ 3.31 = 5.08 Ω/km (0.00508 Ω per metre). In inches: 2.053 ÷ 25.4 = 0.0808 in. Cross-check by geometry: π × (2.053 ÷ 2)² = 3.31 mm² — the table's diameter and area agree.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the resistance different from some tables I have seen?

Sources differ in the resistivity they assume (1.68 vs 1.72 ×10⁻⁸ Ω·m for copper are both common), in temperature (20 °C vs 25 °C), and in whether the conductor is solid or stranded. The differences are a few percent; this page states its constants so you can reproduce every figure.

How do I measure an unknown wire's gauge?

Strip the insulation and measure the conductor diameter with calipers or a micrometer (for stranded wire, measure one strand and multiply its area by the strand count, or use a printed AWG gauge plate). Then use diameter mode here for the nearest gauge.

What do 1/0, 2/0, 3/0 and 4/0 mean?

Below 1 AWG the series continues as 0 (written 1/0), 00 (2/0), 000 (3/0) and 0000 (4/0) — progressively thicker conductors used for service entrances, battery cables and heavy feeders.
